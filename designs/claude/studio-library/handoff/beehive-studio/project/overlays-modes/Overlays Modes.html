03/15 Brief — Overlays, modes & modals

Everything that lives —
on top of the desk.

Prompts 1 and 2 built the room — the walnut shell, the cream pages, the binder and the specialised surfaces inside it. Prompt 3 is everything else: the corkboard you flip the desk over for; the lamp dimming when you call up a modal; the version history that slides in from the right; the sprint timer that finishes with a small ceremony. Eleven surfaces, but the same room.

None of this is new visual language — it's the established system pressed into transient duty. A modal is a paper card lifted off the desk. A drawer is the metadata panel swapped at the seam. The corkboard is the index-card from Prompt 2, full-bleed, pinned to a real ground. The sprint celebration is a single candle and a small toast — not confetti cannons.

Eleven surfaces · five patterns

The brief lists eleven surfaces. Behind them are only five patterns we need to design once and reuse: mode (replaces a region of the shell — corkboard, focus), drawer (panel swapped into an existing slot — version history, writing analysis), overlay (floats over the editor without dimming — find & replace, snapshot banner), modal (centered card with backdrop dim — cheatsheet, export, confirm), popover (small, anchored — sprint setup). Empty states and the sprint celebration aren't patterns; they're the brand's voice expressed at the moments the product is otherwise silent.

01 The taxonomy

Five patterns, one room.

Every transient surface in Beehive falls into one of five buckets. We decide which by answering one question: how much of the room is the user still in? A mode replaces a region; a drawer swaps a slot; an overlay floats; a modal interrupts; a popover whispers. The same paper cards and walnut chrome dress all five — what changes is whether the rest of the studio is still operable.

Rule of thumb. If the action can be done without taking the writer's attention off the prose, it's an overlay. If it needs the writer's whole attention, it's a modal. If it changes what the writer is looking at without losing the binder, it's a mode or a drawer. We earn the user's full attention sparingly — only Confirm / Export / Cheatsheet use the backdrop.

Rule. Drawers sit inside the metadata panel slot at the base chrome layer — they don't elevate, they replace. That's why version-history and analysis can stay live next to the editor without competing with it visually.

Backdrop · one recipe

Modals dim and blur the room beneath them. The same recipe everywhere: rgba(15,12,8,0.62) + 4px blur + 0.85 saturation. The slight desat is what makes the yellow on the modal feel like the new focal point.

03 Motion

Gentle, never snappy.

Three durations — 140, 220, 340ms — paired with one curve: cubic-bezier(.22,1,.36,1), a soft decelerate that lands without overshooting. Nothing in the studio uses linear motion and nothing bounces. Cozy means the room doesn't jump when you open a drawer in it.

05 Surface — Focus mode

Sidebars away —
and the lamp dims, just a little.

Focus mode is the simplest surface in this document and the most opinionated. It hides the binder and metadata panel, keeps the toolbar and status bar visible, and applies one small refinement Chris asked us to consider: a 12% fade on the chrome that isn't the editor. The result is a centered prose column that looks more like a sheet of paper than at any other moment in the studio.

Focus · 0 · 1fr · 0
Transition. On enter, the binder and metadata grid tracks animate to 0 over 340ms with our standard curve. The editor's left/right padding grows to fill the freed space; the toolbar fades 12% (brand chip and the maximize button stay full opacity for affordance). On exit, the same animation in reverse — and if there's a sprint running, the progress strip seamlessly re-anchors to the bottom bar without flickering.

Toolbar in focus mode

The full toolbar stays visible at 88% opacity; on hover anywhere along its row it returns to 100%. The minimize button replaces the maximize, the cheatsheet hint shifts to the centre right, and the lower-priority "Find" / "History" / "Analysis" buttons stay live — Focus is not a kiosk, it's a layout choice.

What we chose against

  • Wider prose. The brief proposes letting the prose stretch. We don't — line length is a typographic choice and 720px is right at 18px Newsreader. Hiding the sidebars just frees the air around it.
  • Hide toolbar on idle. The brief calls this a "minimal sub-mode." We didn't ship it. Writers reach for Bold mid-sentence; the cost of hiding the toolbar is hover discovery on every formatting reach.
  • Hide status bar. Word count + save dot + sprint are productive reassurance. They stay.
  • Tinting the chrome. The remaining chrome is fade-to-88%, not tinted. Tinting the toolbar warmer in focus made it feel like a second app — keeping the same color but reducing presence felt right.

07 Surface — Snapshot preview banner

"You're looking at —
an earlier version."

Click a snapshot in the drawer and the editor enters preview mode. A thin yellow banner appears between the toolbar and the prose body. The prose is the snapshot's content; the writer can read but not type; autosave is paused. Two actions: Restore this version (yellow, primary) and Back to current (ghost). Both are explicit; there is no "click-out to dismiss."

Restore is reversible. Restoring an old snapshot first writes the current live draft as a manual snapshot labelled "before restore," then loads the chosen version into the editor and resumes autosave. So even though the banner copy reads "restore this version," nothing is destroyed — which is why we kept the action yellow (informational primary) rather than red (destructive). The confirmation dialog for restore is the lighter "Are you sure?" variant; see §12.
08 Surface — Find & Replace overlay

A drawer that lives —
at the top of the page.

Same family as VS Code / Google Docs — a thin strip that slides down between the toolbar and the prose, autofocuses the search input, and highlights matches in the body. We slimmed it: no busy icon-button row, no nested popovers. Match case · Whole word · Regex all live in the bar; Replace is a toggle that expands a second row. Light + dark parity is non-negotiable, so we spec both.

09 Surface — Writing analysis panel

Hand-drawn stats —
not a terminal.

Reading level, sentence-length distribution, pacing, adverb / passive / filler counts, and a short list of most-used words. The brief warns against "Bloomberg terminal" charts. We took the warning seriously: histograms feel ruled and ink-stamped, stat rows are paper-coloured cards, the headline score is in Comfortaa not a pie chart. This panel pushes the prose narrower rather than overlaying it — writers want to see both at once.

12 Surface — Confirmation dialogs

Three weights —
destructive, warn, info.

One dialog shape, three icon + button treatments. Destructive uses the --error red for icon + primary button — exclusively for deletion. Warn uses the supporting amber for restore-type "you might want to think first" moments — yellow icon, yellow button (brand). Info uses brand-yellow for happy-path confirms — overwrite this preset, etc. Cancel is always ghost; never compete with the action.

Copy voice. Every confirm dialog is one short sentence + a body sentence that names what's affected by number ("3,240 words", "48 words", "780 words"). Never "this item" or "this content." The body is a writer's room — the dialog uses the writer's currency.

14 Surface — Sprint timer states

A ritual —
with a small ceremony at the end.

Five states, in order: idle (just a Start button — covered in Prompt 1), setup (a small popover anchored to that button), running (a pill with countdown and controls, a yellow strip growing along the top of the status bar), paused (gray pill, resume + stop), finished (a single candle, a small toast, a paper-confetti drift).
