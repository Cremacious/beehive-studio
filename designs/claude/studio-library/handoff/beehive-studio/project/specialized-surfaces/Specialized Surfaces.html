02/15 Brief — Specialized editor surfaces

Different pages —
same beautifully bound book.

A novel's chapters are the room a writer lives in most. But around them sit the other pages that make a manuscript a book: the title page that opens it, the outline that planned it, the notes pinned to the wall while it was written, the character sheets that ran alongside.

This document designs every one of those non-chapter surfaces. They sit inside the same shell from Prompt 1 — binder, toolbar, status bar, metadata — and share its tokens verbatim. Each one is its own page in the writer's notebook, not a settings screen wearing a costume.

Five surfaces · one family

Front & Back Matter become live, WYSIWYG book pages — the title page actually looks like a title page, the dedication sits alone on a sheet centered and italic, the copyright reads as a copyright. No more "Book title:" input next to "Author:" input. Outline trades its generic Kanban for a Beat Sheet — vertical, structured, anchored to act labels. Two alternates ride the same data: a Story Arc plotted along a curve, and a refreshed Corkboard of paper index cards on a peg-board ground. Research Notes get a legal-pad treatment with a paperclip and a red margin rule. Character Profiles become a sectioned writer's notebook page with a portrait, sectional cards, and a relationships block. The Generic fallback is a single cream sheet — a soft holding pen until a proper renderer ships.

What carries through unchanged
  • Three-column shell · 264 / 1fr / 288.
  • Chrome — warm walnut, never slate.
  • Save badge — green dot · yellow dot · pulsing gray.
  • Editor light / dark — paper or coffee canvas.
  • Status pills, popovers, modals, toasts.
  • Type — Comfortaa · Geist · Newsreader · JBM.
What we add
  • Book-page card — taller-than-wide paper.
  • Index-card variant — short, ruled, pinned.
  • Subtype pill bar & chapter-link popover.
  • Tag chips, color swatches, drag handles.
01 The shared pattern

Five renderers, one dispatcher.

Architecturally every specialized surface is a render branch off the active binder item's type. Visually they share a small set of conventions so the user feels they are flipping pages in one book, not opening different apps.

The pane header — every non-chapter surface starts here.

A 56px strip at the top of the center pane. Carries the type-color dot, the type label, the item title, and the save badge. Replaces the chapter toolbar on surfaces where formatting tools aren't relevant.

05 Outline — replace the Kanban

A planning desk —
not a project board.

Kanban works for shipping software. It's the wrong metaphor for a novelist plotting a story. Three columns labelled by act lose the through-line; a card called "Climax" sitting between two cards called "Decision" tells you nothing about what the story does.

We propose one primary view — the Beat Sheet — and two view-mode alternates that ride the same data and ship as toggleable views once primary lands. All three are richer than the Kanban they replace, and all three feel like a writer's room, not a Jira board.

Primary

Beat Sheet

A vertical list of story beats keyed to a structure template (Three-Act default, with Save-the-Cat and Hero's Journey alternates). Each beat is a card with title, description, optional chapter link, tags. Act labels divide the rail.

  • Works for any novel length — short stories to 100k+.
  • Vertical scroll is familiar; reads top-to-bottom like the story will.
  • The connector line down the gutter is the through-line.
  • Tightest data → ink ratio of the three.
Alternate · view-mode

Story Arc

Cards plotted along a literal rising-action curve — beats at lower altitude on the setup side, climax at peak, denouement falling. Best for visual planners who think in shapes.

  • Drag cards laterally to reposition along the chronology.
  • Visual altitude = tension; horizontal = time.
  • Caps practical at ~25 beats — wider grids get crowded.
Alternate · view-mode

Corkboard

Paper index cards on a peg-board ground. Each beat is a ruled card with a yellow pin; the whole grid can be reordered by drag. The most literal "writer's notebook" of the three; great for moodboarding stretches of a draft.

  • Most tactile — leans into the cozy brief.
  • Less precise for sequencing; great for clustering.
  • Reuses paper-card surface from FM/BM.
Recommendation. Ship the Beat Sheet as the default outline view. Wire the data model — title, description, position, chapter-link, tags, status — so the same items render through Story Arc and Corkboard. Add the view switcher as a header control; let the user pick which feels best for their project.

10 Research notes

Legal pad on the desk —
paperclip in the corner.

A note is not a book page. It's the thing pinned to the wall while the book is being written. The chapter editor's prose treatment is too formal here; the FM/BM book page is too iconic. So we give notes a ruled-pad look — red margin rule, faint horizontal lines starting under the title, a paperclip in the top corner. Same cream paper, smaller, less precious.

Three letters survive. 1898, 1903, and 1910. Only the last is dated in his hand — the other two are dated by the recipient on the outside of the envelope. The 1903 letter is the longest. It contains the latitudes that — if I'm right — are the latitudes of the second chart. Verify against the archive catalogue (Mon).

11 Character profile

A writer's notebook page —
not a database row.

Three layouts evaluated (sheet, card grid, narrative). We recommend the Sectioned Card Grid — a portrait header on one cream sheet, then a two-column grid of section cards beneath it. Each card is its own field (Appearance, Personality, Backstory, Arc, Notes, Relationships), draggable to reorder, collapsible. Notes sits full-width at the bottom.

Recommend

Sectioned Card Grid

Each field is a paper card. Section order is the user's. Easy to scan, easy to extend (new section = new card). Works at any depth — a sparse profile shows only the cards the user has filled in; a deep one keeps a long sheet legible.

Sheet style

Closer to a tabletop RPG sheet. One paper rectangle, labeled fields, hand-drawn rules. Stronger metaphor, but stiff — every character looks the same and adding a field costs a lot of layout.

Narrative profile

Free-flowing inline text — section labels in-line with prose, like a Wikipedia article. Cool but discoverability suffers: a user looking up "what colour are her eyes" has to read.

Begins certain that to know the coast is to know everything. Ends knowing that the most important map of her life was made by someone else. Stops finishing her father's chart and starts her own — a coast she has walked, not inherited.

15 Handoff & open questions

What's locked, what's still open.

Same hand-off format as Prompt 1. Locked items feed forward to engineering and to Prompt 3 (overlays / modes). Open questions are for Chris to settle before the corresponding ticket lands.

Locked from this brief
  • FM/BM as live WYSIWYG book pages — one page per subtype; pill-bar picker.
  • Outline ships with the Beat Sheet primary view. Story Arc and Corkboard are view-mode toggles riding the same data.
  • Surface header (replacing the chapter toolbar on non-chapter surfaces) — type chip · name · save badge · contextual actions.
  • Save badge — three states, position is the surface header (FM/BM, Note, Character) or the right side of the outline header (Outline).
  • Tag chips, color label swatch (5), drag handle, drop indicator — new components; tokens unchanged.
